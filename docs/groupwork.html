Group 1 · Small nudges

Why does SCOOT change the lights only a few seconds at a time?

Watch: Morning rush: a fixed plan vs SCOOT’s small, frequent changes.

Investigate
  1. What are the split, the cycle and the offset? Explain each in one line.
  2. Count the timing decisions SCOOT makes at one junction in one hour (60 s cycle, two stages).
  3. What could go wrong if SCOOT made one big change instead of many small ones?
Fundamentals:F1F6F7F8

Look in: Section 1 · website: What SCOOT is

Report back: one-sentence answer · one key number · explain the animation
Show the answer: Small nudges, many times an hour
  • Split = how the green is shared between stages. Cycle = time to run all stages once. Offset = timing between neighbouring junctions.
  • SCOOT tries a small change before every stage change (split), once per cycle (offset) and every 5 minutes (cycle).
  • Decisions per hour: 120 split + 60 offset + 12 cycle = about 192.
  • Each nudge is a few seconds, so one wrong guess cannot do much harm; together they follow the traffic, like a thermostat.
≈ 192timing decisions per hour at one junction
DiscussAsk: why not just pick the best plan once each morning? (Traffic changes minute by minute; a stored plan is out of date by the time the peak arrives.)

Group 2 · Detectors & LPUs

Why is SCOOT’s detector far up the road, and why does a bus count more than a car?

Watch: A platoon is counted just after the upstream junction, long before the stop line.

Investigate
  1. Where is the SCOOT loop, and what does SCOOT gain from putting it there?
  2. What is an LPU? Write the rule used in this lab.
  3. Work out the LPUs for a car (4.5 m) and a bus (12 m) at 13.5 m/s over a 2 m loop.
Fundamentals:F2F3

Look in: Section 2 · website: Detectors and LPUs

Report back: one-sentence answer · one key number · explain the animation
Show the answer: Early warning, and a fair measure of traffic
  • The loop sits just after the upstream junction, so SCOOT learns about cars 10–20 s before they reach the stop line and can get the green ready.
  • An LPU (link profile unit) mixes a count with how long the loop is covered: LPU = 1 × vehicle + 2 × seconds covered.
  • Car: (4.5 + 2) ÷ 13.5 = 0.48 s → 1.96 LPU. Bus: (12 + 2) ÷ 13.5 = 1.04 s → 3.07 LPU.
  • Long or slow vehicles cover the loop longer and need more green, so they score more.
1.96 vs 3.07LPUs for a car and a bus
DiscussAsk: what happens to a car’s LPU if it crawls over the loop in a jam? (Covered longer, more LPUs: an early sign of congestion.)

Group 3 · Platoons spread

A platoon passes the detector in 10 s. Why does it reach the stop line spread over about 25 s?

Watch: Fast drivers pull ahead, slow drivers fall behind on the way to the stop line.

Investigate
  1. What is a cyclic flow profile?
  2. Why do the first cars arrive after 0.8 × cruise time, not the full cruise time?
  3. What does F = 0.263 mean? What happens to F on a longer road?
Fundamentals:F2F3F7

Look in: Section 3 · website: Profiles and dispersion

Report back: one-sentence answer · one key number · explain the animation
Show the answer: Shift, then smear
  • A cyclic flow profile is the LPUs seen in each second of the cycle, averaged over several cycles.
  • Shift: the fastest drivers lead, so arrivals start at 0.8 × T = 8 s (T = 10 s cruise time).
  • Smear: each second, 26% of the new traffic arrives and 74% of last second’s arrivals carry over (F = 1 ÷ (1 + 0.35 × 0.8 × 10) = 0.263).
  • Same 10 LPU, lower peak (0.95), about 25 s long. Longer road → smaller F (T = 20 s gives 0.15) → more spread.
F = 0.263share of each second’s traffic that arrives first
DiscussAsk: on a long road, does coordinating the two signals still help much? (Less: the platoon has smeared into steady flow.)

Group 4 · Queue & DoS

SCOOT never sees the queue. How does it know whether a green is long enough?

Watch: The bathtub: red fills the queue, green drains it.

Investigate
  1. Explain the queue model with a bathtub: what are the tap, the plug and the plughole?
  2. Arrivals 0.2 LPU/s, red 35 s, green 25 s, SATO 0.6 LPU/s: how big is the queue at the end of red, and when does it clear?
  3. Work out the degree of saturation (DoS). Why aim for about 90%, not 100%?
Fundamentals:F1F3F4F5F6

Look in: Section 4 · website: The queue model

Report back: one-sentence answer · one key number · explain the animation
Show the answer: A model queue, drained by the green
  • SCOOT models the queue from the upstream counts: arrivals are the tap, red is the plug, and SATO is the size of the plughole.
  • End of red: 0.2 × 35 = 7 LPU. In green it shrinks at 0.6 − 0.2 = 0.4 LPU/s, so it clears after 7 ÷ 0.4 = 17.5 s.
  • DoS = demand ÷ what the green can clear = (0.2 × 60) ÷ (25 × 0.6) = 12 ÷ 15 = 0.80.
  • Aiming for about 90% leaves a margin for random surges. Above 100% the queue grows every cycle.
DoS 0.80under the 90% target: spare green
DiscussAsk: if this link has spare green, where should SCOOT give it? (To the busiest stage at the junction, a few seconds at a time.)

Group 5 · Validation

The model says the queue clears in 32 s; the engineer’s stopwatch says 36 s. Who is right, and what do you change?

Watch: A queue drains across the stop line at the saturation occupancy (SATO).

Investigate
  1. What is SATO, and why can it differ from street to street?
  2. Queue 19.5 LPU (10 cars), start lag 3 s, observed clear time 36 s: what should SATO be?
  3. Name two other settings an engineer checks when validating a link.
Fundamentals:F2F4F6

Look in: Section 5 · website: Validation

Report back: one-sentence answer · one key number · explain the animation
Show the answer: The street is right: tune the model
  • SATO is how fast a standing queue drains in green, in LPU per second. It depends on lane width, gradient, turning traffic and parking.
  • The street is always right. SATO = queue ÷ (clear time − start lag) = 19.5 ÷ (36 − 3) = 0.59 LPU/s (the model had 0.67).
  • Other settings: journey (cruise) time, start and end lags, and the maximum queue clear time.
  • Like tuning a guitar: a well-built model still plays wrong notes until it is tuned to the street.
0.59 LPU/sSATO after tuning (was 0.67)
DiscussAsk: what goes wrong if SATO is set too high? (The model thinks queues clear sooner, so SCOOT gives too little green and real queues grow.)

Group 6 · SCOOT vs others

Is SCOOT always the best way to run traffic lights?

Watch: One junction, the same arrivals, three ways to run the lights (Lab F3).

Investigate
  1. How do fixed time, actuated and SCOOT signals each decide the green?
  2. Compare the mean delay of the three controllers in Lab F3. By how much does SCOOT win?
  3. When might a fixed plan or simple actuation be good enough?
Fundamentals:F1F5F8

Look in: Section 6 · website: Live junction

Report back: one-sentence answer · one key number · explain the animation
Show the answer: Usually better, not always
  • Fixed time follows a stored plan. Actuated extends the green while cars keep arriving at the stop line. SCOOT predicts from upstream and coordinates junctions.
  • Lab F3, one junction: 18.96 / 14.48 / 12.79 s mean delay, so SCOOT has 33% less delay than fixed time and 12% less than actuation.

  • A simple controller can be enough at isolated, lightly used junctions. SCOOT needs working detectors and a validated model.

Fundamentals for every group

Nine short building blocks before you start. Each group needs only some of them: the chips on every slide tell you which.

F1What a traffic signal controls

A signal shares one road space between movements that would collide. It gives each group of movements a turn, over and over, like a teacher letting one row of students out at a time.

  • Stage: movements that get green together (for example the main road, then the side road).
  • Cycle C: the time to run every stage once, for example 60 s.
  • Intergreen: amber + all-red between stages; partly lost time, when nobody moves well.
  • Split: how the green is shared, for example 26 s for the main road and 24 s for the side road.
Try the numbers60 s cycle = 26 s green A + 5 s intergreen + 24 s green B + 5 s intergreen.

F2A road network in SCOOT words

SCOOT sees a town as junctions joined by one-way approach roads. Each approach has its own detector and its own little model.

  • Node: a signalled junction.
  • Link: one approach road, from its detector to its stop line.
  • Platoon: a bunch of cars released together by the upstream green.
  • Cruise time T: how long a car takes from the detector to the stop line, for example 10 s.
Try the numbersA 140 m link driven at 14 m/s (50 km/h) has a cruise time of 140 ÷ 14 = 10 s.
For groups235

F3Measuring traffic: flow, headway, occupancy

Three ways to describe the same stream of cars: how many pass, how far apart they are in time, and how much of the time the detector is covered.

  • Flow q: vehicles per hour (veh/h) or per second.
  • Headway: seconds between one car and the next; headway = 1 ÷ flow.
  • Occupancy: share of time the detector is covered. Long or slow vehicles cover it longer.
  • SCOOT’s LPU mixes the count with the covered time (group 2).
Try the numbers540 veh/h = 540 ÷ 3600 = 0.15 veh/s, so one car every 1 ÷ 0.15 ≈ 6.7 s.
For groups234

F4Saturation flow and capacity

When the light turns green, a standing queue leaves at a steady top speed, one car about every 2 s per lane. That top rate is the saturation flow. SCOOT measures the same idea in LPU per second and calls it SATO.

  • Saturation flow s: about 1800 veh/h per lane (1 car every 2 s) on a good lane.
  • Start lag: the first cars take a few seconds to get going.
  • Capacity = s × g ÷ C: what the approach can carry per hour.
  • Narrow lanes, hills, parking and turning traffic lower s.
Try the numberss = 1800 veh/h, green g = 25 s, cycle C = 60 s: capacity = 1800 × 25 ÷ 60 = 750 veh/h.
For groups45

F5Queues, delay and stops

During red, arriving cars pile up. During green the queue drains faster than cars arrive, so it shrinks. Waiting time is delay; every car that had to brake to a halt is a stop.

  • Queue grows in red at the arrival rate.
  • In green it shrinks at saturation flow − arrival rate.
  • Delay = area under the queue line (vehicle-seconds).
  • Stops = cars arriving before the queue clears.
Try the numbersArrivals 0.15 veh/s, red 35 s, s = 0.5 veh/s: queue 0.15 × 35 = 5.25 veh, clears after 5.25 ÷ 0.35 = 15 s of green.
For groups46

F6Degree of saturation: is the green big enough?

Compare what arrives with what the green can clear. A number below 1 means spare green; above 1 means the queue grows every cycle.

  • DoS = demand ÷ capacity.
  • Below about 0.9: comfortable, room for random surges.
  • About 1.0: the green is just used up.
  • Above 1.0: queues grow every cycle, whatever the timing.
Try the numbersDemand 540 veh/h, capacity 750 veh/h: DoS = 540 ÷ 750 = 0.72 (72%).
For groups145

F7Coordinating junctions: offsets and green waves

Neighbouring signals can be timed so a platoon released by one arrives at the next just as it turns green. The time between their greens is the offset.

  • Offset: time from green at one junction to green at the next.
  • Good offset ≈ travel time between them, so the platoon meets a green.
  • Neighbours must share a cycle (a region) to keep the wave.
  • The wave works best when platoons stay tight (group 3).
Try the numbersJunctions 300 m apart at 15 m/s: travel time 20 s, so set the second green about 20 s after the first.
For groups13

F8Three ways to run a signal

All signals share the road; they differ in how they decide the green.

  • Fixed time: Runs a stored plan, often made off-line with Webster or TRANSYT. Cheap and predictable, but cannot react.
  • Actuated: Loops at the stop line extend the green while cars keep coming, and end it on a gap. Reacts, but only locally.
  • Adaptive (SCOOT): Loops upstream feed a model; small, frequent changes to split, cycle and offset across a region.
Try the numbersRule of thumb: the more traffic changes and the closer the junctions, the more an adaptive system can help.
