    Reading the degree of saturation

    • What it is. DS = traffic arriving in one cycle ÷ what the green could discharge in that cycle. Both are measured in LPUs, so it needs a validated saturation occupancy to be right.
    • How to read it. Below 80%: spare green. Around 90%: busy but working, and the level SCOOT aims for at the busiest node. Above 100%: more traffic arrives than can leave, so the queue grows every cycle.
    • How SCOOT uses it. The split optimiser moves green towards the stage with the higher DS. The cycle optimiser finds the node with the highest DS in a region, the critical node, and changes the cycle to hold it near 90%.
    • Why it can read too high. When a queue reaches back over the detector, the loop stays covered and the LPU count jumps. That is one reason the detector must sit beyond the normal queue.

    How to read this animation

    • It is a teaching simulation. The road is an invented waterfront corridor in the style of a Gulf corniche. Its layout, traffic volumes and results are illustrative. They are not a survey or a forecast of any real road.
    • Every car drives itself. Each driver picks a speed, follows the car ahead, changes lane and stops for red, so queues and platoons form on their own. Nothing on screen is a pre-drawn path.
    • The controller is SCOOT-style, not SCOOT. It follows the published principles: upstream detectors read four times a second, a cyclic flow profile, a model queue, and three optimisers that move splits by 1 to 4 s, offsets by 4 s and the cycle by 4 or 8 s. The LPU coding and all parameter values are simplified.
    • The comparison is fair but small. Both streets get the same vehicles from the same random seed. The fixed plan is set for eastbound traffic at normal volumes, so it suffers when the rush arrives from the other direction. A fixed plan retimed for the rush would do better.
